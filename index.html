Trade Karma

Smart Money material is famously hard to read — not because the ideas are complicated, but because it is delivered in a private vocabulary. This site translates that vocabulary into ordinary language, one concept at a time.

Every page here answers three questions about a concept: what it actually is, why anyone claims it works, and what you would have to see on a chart to say it is present. Where a picture helps, there is a diagram. Where the jargon is the only obstacle, there is a plain sentence and a glossary entry.

Read this first

This is educational writing about a trading methodology, not advice and not a recommendation to trade anything. The ideas described here are one community's model of how markets move; they are not established fact, and none of them are backed by an audited track record. Most people who day-trade lose money. Nothing on this site should be treated as a reason to risk capital.

The one-paragraph version

The whole framework rests on a single claim: price does not wander, it is delivered. Large participants need a counterparty to fill size, so price is walked to places where lots of resting orders sit — mostly the stop-losses parked above obvious highs and below obvious lows. Once those orders are absorbed, price moves away, often leaving behind untraded gaps that it later returns to fill. Everything else in the vocabulary is a name for either a place where orders are resting (liquidity), a gap or level price tends to revisit (a PD array), or a time of day when this is most likely to happen (a killzone).

How the vocabulary is organised

It helps enormously to know that every term belongs to one of four buckets. When you meet a new acronym, decide which bucket it is in first — you will usually find you already understand what it does.

BucketWhat it namesExamples
Liquidity A place where resting orders are believed to sit, and therefore a place price is drawn toward. BSL, SSL, EQH/EQL, PDH/PDL, ERL, IRL, DOL
PD array A price level or zone where a reaction is expected — usually an inefficiency to be refilled or a candle body to be revisited. FVG, OB, BB, MB, RB, BPR, NWOG, liquidity void
Time A window in the day or week when the above are expected to be acted on. LOKZ, NYOKZ, LCKZ, AKZ, CBDR, silver bullet
Structure Descriptions of the shape price has already drawn, used to decide direction. STH/STL, ITH/ITL, LTH/LTL, MSS, BOS, displacement

A working order of study

  1. Liquidity first. If you do not accept that stop orders are the target, none of the rest has a motive behind it.
  2. Then the arrays. Fair value gaps and order blocks account for the large majority of entries. The exotic blocks are variations on those two.
  3. Then structure. This is what stops you from taking a perfectly valid array in the wrong direction.
  4. Then time. The same level behaves differently at 3am and at 3pm. Time is the filter that turns a long list of levels into a short list of candidates.
  5. Models last. A model is just a fixed order in which you check the four things above. Learning the model before the pieces produces confident nonsense.
Honest caveat

Much of this framework is unfalsifiable as stated. "Price seeks liquidity or rebalances an inefficiency" covers both directions of every move, so it can never be wrong after the fact. That does not make the individual observations useless — session timing and prior-day levels are real, measurable things — but it does mean the framework cannot be evaluated by how well it explains charts you are looking at backwards. Only forward testing with recorded rules tells you anything.

Liquidity

Liquidity is the whole engine. Strip the jargon away and the claim is simple: big orders need someone to trade against, and the most reliable supply of willing counterparties is other traders' stop-losses.

What "liquidity" actually means here

In normal finance, liquidity means how easily you can buy or sell without moving the price. In this framework the word is used more narrowly. Liquidity means a cluster of resting orders at a known price — mostly stop-losses, plus breakout orders — that will convert into market orders the moment price touches them.

That conversion is the point. A stop-loss is not a passive request; it is a pending market order pointed the opposite way to the position it protects. A crowd of long positions with stops under an obvious low is, from a large seller's point of view, a crowd of guaranteed buyers sitting at a known address. Price only has to go and ring the bell.

The mechanic, spelled out

A trader is long. Their stop sits below a recent low. When price trades through that low the stop fires as a sell at market. Multiply by thousands of accounts and you get a burst of selling at a known price — which is exactly what someone trying to buy a large position needs. This is why the framework insists that price moves toward obvious levels rather than respecting them.

Buyside and sellside

The naming trips everyone up at first, because it is named after the orders that fire, not after where they sit relative to price.

TermWhere it sitsWhat is resting thereWho wants it
BSL Buyside liquidity Above highs Stops of short positions, and breakout buy orders Someone who needs to sell size
SSL Sellside liquidity Below lows Stops of long positions, and breakdown sell orders Someone who needs to buy size

So "price is going for buyside" means price is expected to rally through a high — and the implication is bearish afterwards, because the entity that just filled its short book has no further reason to hold price up.

Where the pools are, ranked

Not all liquidity is equal. The framework grades it roughly by the timeframe that produced it, because bigger timeframes attract more participants and therefore more stops.

  • Major — highs and lows of the day, week, month, quarter and year. This is what a higher-timeframe bias is built on.
  • Medium — highs and lows visible on the 15-minute and 1-hour charts. This is the working range for a day trader.
  • Minor — highs and lows on 1- to 5-minute charts. Used for timing, not for direction.

Layered on top of the timeframe ranking are the specific formations that concentrate orders:

  • Equal highs / equal lows (EQH / EQL) — two or more touches at nearly the same price. The flattest, most obvious levels attract the most stops.
  • Previous day's high and low (PDH / PDL) — the single most-watched pair of levels in intraday trading, and the easiest daily target to name in advance.
  • Previous week's high and low (PWH / PWL) — the same idea one timeframe up.
  • Session highs and lows — the extremes of the Asian, London and New York windows. See time.
  • Trendline touches — every trader who draws the same diagonal places stops just beyond it, which quietly stacks orders along a slanted line.
  • Range boundaries — in a sideways market, above the top and below the bottom.

Internal vs external range liquidity

This is the single most useful idea on the page, and the one that makes daily bias tractable. Take any range you can see — a swing low to a swing high. Then:

  • External range liquidity (ERL) = the old high and the old low that define the range. Stops live outside them.
  • Internal range liquidity (IRL) = the inefficiencies inside the range — chiefly fair value gaps and order blocks — that price has not yet come back to fill.

How to actually use it

Mark the monthly, weekly and daily ranges. Ask one question: was the last thing that happened an external raid or an internal fill? If a high was just taken, look for the nearest unfilled gap below and treat that as the next objective. If a gap was just filled, look for the old high or low that is now most exposed. You are not predicting; you are naming the next stop on a two-stop loop.

Draw on liquidity (DOL)

"Draw on liquidity" is simply the level you believe price is currently travelling toward. It is the destination, not the entry. Everything else is negotiation about how to get on board.

Candidates for a day trader, roughly in order of usefulness: previous day's high or low; previous week's high or low; the high or low of the Asian, London or New York session; an obvious set of equal highs or equal lows; a large unfilled gap on the 1-hour or 4-hour chart.

The discipline that matters more than the choice: having named a draw, do not change it every fifteen minutes. The most common self-inflicted wound in this methodology is flip-flopping the destination each time a five-minute candle goes the other way.

High-resistance and low-resistance runs

Two liquidity pools can look identical and behave completely differently, depending on whether anything stands between price and the pool.

Low-resistance run (LRLR)High-resistance run (HRLR)
What it is Untouched orders with a clear path to them Orders that price must fight through opposing structure to reach
How it feels Fast, one-sided, few pullbacks Grinding, choppy, long time in the trade
Typical shape A series of higher lows leading up to untouched highs A high that already ran a previous high and rejected
Verdict What you want to target What you want to avoid targeting

The practical test is boring and effective: has this level already been swept once? Liquidity that has been taken is spent. A high that has already run a previous high and reversed is now high-resistance — the incentive to go back for it is gone. Untouched equal highs sitting above a staircase of higher lows are low-resistance, and that is where the framework says to aim.

Stop hunts, Judas swings and turtle soup

These three terms all describe the same event seen from slightly different angles.

Stop hunt

The generic name. Price moves to a level specifically to trigger the orders resting there, then leaves. If you have ever been stopped out to the tick and then watched price go your original way, you have met one.

Judas swing

A stop hunt with a time attached. It is the first move of a session or a day, in the direction opposite to where the day actually goes — the false start that establishes the day's high on a down day, or the day's low on an up day. The name is a betrayal joke: the move that looks like the start of a trend is the one that sells you out.

Where it usually appears: the London open raiding the high or low of the Asian range; the New York open around 9:30 taking a short-term high or low before reversing; or the first move after a scheduled news release. If big news lands at 8:30, the 9:30 fake-out often does not happen — the manipulation already occurred at the news.

Turtle soup

The trade you take against the false breakout. Price pokes just beyond an obvious old high or low, fails to hold, and closes back inside. You enter on the failure, with a stop beyond the extreme of the poke. It is called soup because it eats the breakout traders — the "turtles" being an older generation of breakout-following speculators.

Where this reasoning gets dangerous

"It was a stop hunt" is available as an explanation for any losing trade, which makes it psychologically toxic. A breakout that fails is a stop hunt; a breakout that runs is "displacement". You cannot tell which you are in until afterwards. The only protection is to define, before entry, what price would have to do to prove you wrong — and then honour it.

Liquidity as an entry, liquidity as an exit

A rule worth internalising, because it quietly organises everything else:

Enter on internal liquidity. Exit on external liquidity.
You take your position inside the range, at a gap or an order block. You take profit outside the range, at the old high or old low that everyone can see. Doing it the other way around — buying the breakout above the obvious high, selling the breakdown below the obvious low — is the behaviour the whole model is built to exploit.

The corollary is a blunt pair of rules the material repeats often: do not buy above an old high, and do not sell below an old low. Not because breakouts never work, but because at those prices you are, by construction, providing the liquidity rather than using it.

Liquidity voids

A liquidity void is a stretch of price covered in one direction so quickly that almost no two-way trading happened there — on the chart, a run of long-bodied candles with tiny wicks. The claim is that these ranges are unfinished business: price spent no time there, so both buying and selling still need to be offered at those prices, and the market tends to come back and do it.

A void is not the same thing as a fair value gap, though they usually appear together. A fair value gap is a precise three-candle formation with defined edges; a void is the broader stretch of one-sided delivery around it. Voids are used more for targets — "there is unfinished business back there" — and gaps more for entries. Both are covered in detail on the PD arrays page.

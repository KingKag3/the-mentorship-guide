Models

A model is not a secret. It is a fixed order in which you check things you already understand — liquidity, an array, structure, and time — so that you stop improvising under pressure. If the previous four pages made sense, none of what follows is new.

The 2022 entry model

The most widely copied sequence in the whole methodology, and a good template for how these things are built. Six conditions, in order:

  1. Liquidity is taken. Price trades through an obvious pool — the Asian high or low, the London high or low, the previous day's high or low.
  2. Price reverses with displacement. Fast, decisive, leaving a fair value gap behind. No gap means no trade; the gap is the evidence that something serious happened.
  3. A structural break lines up with the gap. A swing high or low sits horizontally level with one of the three candles forming the gap. That break is the market structure shift. It may occur either before or after the liquidity grab — both count.
  4. Measure the displacement range. Draw from the swing that took the liquidity to the swing on the other side of the gap, and mark its 50% level.
  5. Check the gap sits on the right side. The gap should fall at or beyond the midpoint — the side nearer the liquidity that was just taken. A gap on the wrong half of the range is a worse entry.
  6. Place a limit order inside the gap, at the midpoint or beyond, with the stop past the extreme of the raid, and a target at the opposing pool of liquidity.
The short version

Liquidity grab → displacement → structure shift → gap or block beyond the 50% of the displacement leg → limit order on the return → exit at the opposite liquidity. Every other model on this page is a variation on that skeleton.

The ATM method

A standalone pattern usually framed on the 1-hour chart, and simpler to spot than the 2022 model. Bearish version:

  • Find a key high that forms after price has been pushing higher, ideally with a couple of swing highs already broken on the way up.
  • Wait for the short-term low that preceded that high to be broken to the downside — that break is the signal.
  • Wait for price to retrace back into the breaker zone at that broken low.
  • Enter there. Stop above the key high or above the breaker. Target below the next short-term low down.

The bullish version is the mirror: a key low, a broken swing high above it, a pullback into the breaker, entry with the stop below the low.

The market maker buy and sell curves

The most elaborate structure in the methodology, and the one that ties everything together. It describes a complete round trip: a position being built, walked to a target, and unloaded.

How to read it

  1. Establish the higher-timeframe direction. A sell model is used when the daily or 4-hour picture is bearish. The rally is the retracement, not the trend.
  2. Find the initial consolidation. This is the point of origin, and the sell stops that build up beneath it are the eventual target.
  3. Name the destination of the rally. Ideally an old high with a higher-timeframe level sitting just above it, so that one move both takes liquidity and fills an inefficiency.
  4. Grade the range in quarters from origin to destination. Accumulation clusters near the bottom quarter; re-accumulation around the middle; the final push is fast and gives no comfortable pullback.
  5. Wait for the reversal. The preferred version makes a new high above the old one, then breaks a prior swing low with displacement and a gap. A weaker version fails to make the new high and simply rolls over.
  6. Trade the return trip using the 2022 model on the way down, taking entries at the gap left by the break, then at the breaker inside the last stage of accumulation.

The buy model is the same diagram flipped: a decline that engineers buy stops above it and runs sell stops below an old low, then a reversal and a rally back through the initial consolidation. The catchphrase for it — "the market goes lower to go higher" — is a fair summary.

Two useful sub-ideas

  • Change in state of delivery. The moment the algorithm switches from a buy program to a sell program. Concretely: price trades through the opening price of the last up-close candle that ran the liquidity, and leaves a gap doing it. That candle becomes the order block.
  • Measuring gap at 50%. An unfilled gap around the midpoint of the range is treated as confirmation that you measured the range correctly — the halfway marker between origin and destination.

Silver bullet

A narrow, time-boxed application of the same skeleton. You wait for a specific one-hour window, take the first clean gap that forms after a liquidity raid, and target the nearest opposing pool. The most-used window is 10:00–11:00, after the opening-range noise has resolved and while London close volume is still present.

Its appeal is entirely in the constraint. One hour, one setup, one target, and if it does not appear you do nothing. That constraint is doing more work than the pattern is.

Turtle soup

The reversal trade against a failed breakout, covered on the liquidity page. As a model:

  • Identify an obvious old high or low — ideally equal highs or equal lows, with a higher-timeframe level just beyond.
  • Wait for price to poke through and fail: no displacement, no close beyond, back inside quickly.
  • Enter on the return inside, stop beyond the extreme of the poke, target the opposite side of the range.

A specific variant worth knowing: if a short-term low is taken out twice without any move to the upside in between, that second sweep is a strong turtle soup buy candidate.

Daily templates

Rather than a single setup, these are shapes a whole day tends to take. Recognising which one you are in tells you what to expect for the rest of the session.

TemplateShapeHow to trade it
Classic trend day Price moves away from the daily open during London, sets the day's extreme there, and trends for seven or eight hours. The best template. On a buy day, buy the dip below the opening price at a discount level during London; take partial profit into 11:00–12:00.
London swing, New York reversal Starts like a trend day, then reverses around the New York open or London close and returns to clear the early extreme. Watch the last hour of London and the first hour of New York for the turn. Typically happens when price reaches an untouched higher-timeframe level.
Consolidation, then afternoon trend The morning goes nowhere; the move arrives after lunch. Do not force the morning. Use the 13:30 opening price the same way you would use midnight.
Morning trend, afternoon reversal The morning move is retraced from about 13:30 onward. Take profit before lunch rather than holding for a bigger number.
Morning trend, afternoon continuation The afternoon repeats the morning move at roughly the same size. Measure the morning leg and project it from the lunch low or high.
Consolidation raid on news Range before the release, a spike through one side to trigger stops, then the real move the other way. Know your level beyond the range beforehand. If price does not reject within about five minutes of the release, the read was wrong — leave.

The high-probability day trade checklist

Consolidating everything into the conditions the framework wants stacked before it commits. For a long — invert everything for a short:

  • The daily or 4-hour direction is up, and price has reacted positively at a discount level.
  • There is a clear, unobstructed path to a premium level above.
  • It is Monday, Tuesday or Wednesday.
  • The overnight ranges are compressed — an Asian range in the 10–15 point region on index futures, and a central bank dealers range under about 20 points.
  • The window is 02:00–04:00 for London, or the New York morning after 07:20.
  • The entry is at a discount array: a gap below a short-term low from the previous New York session, a bullish block below a short-term low, a projection off the Asian range or dealers range, or a turtle soup on a second sweep.
  • Execution on the 5- or 15-minute chart. Below that is timing, not analysis.

Risk, sizing and exits

The framework's own risk rules are more conservative than its reputation suggests, and this is the part beginners skip.

  • Define the stop by structure, not by comfort. It goes beyond the extreme of the raid you traded against. If that distance is too large for your account, the answer is a smaller position or no trade — not a tighter stop.
  • Do not rush to move it. Trailing a stop up to break-even at the first sign of profit converts winning trades into scratches, because the retracement into the entry zone is a normal part of the pattern.
  • Scale out at pre-named places, not at pre-named profits: the previous day's high or low, the midpoint of the range you are trading inside, a projected deviation of the overnight range, and time-based points such as 10:00–11:00.
  • Aim small. The explicit target size is modest — a handful of points on index futures — rather than the whole move. Consistency comes from repeatable small objectives.
  • One bias per session. If you are bearish, take only shorts. Flip-flopping mid-session is the most reliable way to lose on both sides of the same move.
The part that is not optional

Every point value quoted in this methodology comes from index futures during a particular volatility regime. A "5-point stop" is meaningless on another instrument, and can be meaningless on the same instrument two years later. Convert everything to a fraction of the current average daily range before you use it, and size positions off account risk rather than off a point count someone else wrote down.

Intermarket confirmation

Two correlations that get used as a sanity check on index futures:

  • Dollar index against equity index futures — usually inverse. A rising dollar generally coincides with heavier index futures; a falling or consolidating dollar supports them. When the two stop behaving inversely, take that as a signal that conditions are unreliable rather than as a trade.
  • One index against another — the S&P, Nasdaq and Dow futures should make matching highs and lows. When one makes a new high and another does not, that failure is the SMT divergence described on the structure page, and it is the standard confirmation for a reversal at a level you had already identified.

Journaling, which is where the edge actually comes from

The recommended record is not a profit-and-loss log. For each day, capture three things:

  1. Where displacement occurred, and from which level.
  2. Which clean highs or lows were neutralised, and when.
  3. How price reacted at the swing points in between.

Then, for the week: which day formed the weekly high or low, and in which killzone. Repeated over months, that record is what turns a list of definitions into something you can actually recognise in real time. There is no shortcut past it, and no model on this page substitutes for it.

A closing caution

Every model here is described in terms of what a chart looked like afterwards. That is exactly the condition under which patterns appear reliable and are not. Before risking anything, write the rules down as testable statements — specific window, specific level, specific invalidation — and check them forward against data you have not already seen. If a rule cannot be written down that precisely, it is not a rule.

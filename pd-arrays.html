PD arrays

"PD array" stands for premium/discount array. It is a catalogue term for any specific price level where a reaction is expected. Once you know that, the long list of block types stops being intimidating — they are all answers to the same question: which exact price do I care about?

First: premium, discount and equilibrium

Everything in this catalogue is graded relative to a range. Take a swing low and a swing high, put a line at the midpoint, and you have the whole framework:

  • Equilibrium (EQ) — the 50% level. Fair value. Coin-flip territory.
  • Premium — above the midpoint. Expensive. Where you look to sell.
  • Discount — below the midpoint. Cheap. Where you look to buy.

The rule is refreshingly simple: buy in discount, sell in premium, and be suspicious of anything you want to do at equilibrium. The complications are all about which range you measure. A price can be at a discount on the 5-minute chart and deep in premium on the daily. When those disagree, the higher timeframe wins.

The dealing range

A dealing range is a range whose both ends have already been taken — liquidity swept above the high and below the low. Once that has happened, you have a stable container to measure premium and discount inside, and the arrays within it become meaningful. Before both sides are taken, you are still guessing where the range ends.

Fair value gaps

The workhorse. A fair value gap (FVG) is a three-candle formation where the middle candle moves so hard that the first candle's wick and the third candle's wick do not overlap. The untouched space between them is the gap.

BISI and SIBI

Two acronyms for the same object seen from either side, and they are worth decoding once because they appear constantly:

  • BISI — buyside imbalance, sellside inefficiency. A gap created by a fast move up. Buying happened; selling did not. It sits below price and is a bullish (discount) array.
  • SIBI — sellside imbalance, buyside inefficiency. A gap created by a fast move down. It sits above price and is a bearish (premium) array.

How gaps are used

  • As an entry. Place a limit order inside the gap, typically at or beyond the midpoint, and let price come to you.
  • As a target. An unfilled gap is unfinished business; price is expected to reach for it.
  • As a signal. A gap appearing after a liquidity raid is treated as evidence that the raid was real, because the size of the move needed to create it implies serious participation rather than drift.

Consequent encroachment

An intimidating phrase for "the middle of the gap". Whenever a gap or void is drawn, its 50% line is marked, and reactions are expected there as often as at the edges. If you see "CE" in someone's chart annotation, they mean the midpoint.

Order blocks

An order block is the last candle of the opposite colour before a decisive move.

  • Bullish order block (+OB) — the last down-close candle before a strong rally. Watched as support on the way back down.
  • Bearish order block (−OB) — the last up-close candle before a strong decline. Watched as resistance on the way back up.

The story attached to it: a large participant had to fill their position somewhere, and that candle is where the average price of that position sits. If price returns there, they can add or manage without being underwater — so the level is defended.

What makes a block worth trading

  • It is validated: a later candle trades through the top of the down-close candle (for a bullish block) or the bottom of the up-close candle (for a bearish one).
  • It sits at a level that matters anyway — a session high or low, a previous day's extreme, a higher-timeframe zone.
  • It pushed price into liquidity: the candle itself drove price into a pool of stops.
  • It has a fair value gap paired with it. This is the single best filter in the whole catalogue. A block with a gap attached is treated as high-probability; a block with nothing else going on is a line on a chart.
  • Price left it quickly and did not come straight back. Blocks that get retested immediately usually fail.
The honest problem with order blocks

Every strong move has a "last opposite candle", so on any chart you can find dozens of them, and most do nothing. In isolation they have no predictive content — a fact the source material states directly. They only carry weight once direction has been decided on a higher timeframe and a liquidity objective has been named. Identify the destination first; use the block for timing.

The variations on an order block

These are the terms that make the vocabulary look bigger than it is. Each one is an order block in a particular situation.

Breaker block

An order block that failed, and then flipped sides. Sequence for a bullish breaker: price makes a high, pulls back, then breaks below the prior low (running the stops there), and then rallies back above that high. The down-close candles at the earlier swing high — which were supposed to be resistance — become support on the retest.

A bearish breaker is the mirror: high → low → higher high → lower low, and the zone becomes resistance. Note that despite the drama of the name, a breaker is not necessarily a reversal signal — it very often marks a continuation.

Mitigation block

Almost identical to a breaker, with one difference that is worth memorising because it is the only thing separating them:

Breaker = the swing succeeded. Price made a new extreme and took the liquidity there before reversing.
Mitigation block = the swing failed. Price did not reach the previous extreme — it made a lower high (or higher low) and reversed anyway.

Same trade, weaker premise: because no stops were run, the reversal has less obvious sponsorship behind it. The name refers to trapped traders "mitigating" — getting out at break-even — when price returns to where they entered.

Rejection block

Built from wicks, not bodies. When a swing high forms with long upper wicks, the space between the highest body and the wick tops is the rejection block: price reached up there, grabbed the stops, and was pushed straight back. It is used when the extreme is spiky rather than a clean candle body. The bullish version is the mirror at a swing low with long lower wicks.

Propulsion block

An order block sitting inside an older order block. Price came down into an existing bullish block, formed a fresh down-close candle there, and continued higher. That inner candle becomes a very sensitive level — the framework expects it to hold above its own midpoint, and treats a close beyond that as invalidation.

Reclaimed order block

An old block that price ignored on the first visit but respected later, once the higher timeframe caught up. Treated as evidence that positions were rebuilt at an old level rather than a new one.

Balanced price range / redelivered rebalanced array

A stretch where price went one way, came back the other way over the same prices, and then went the first way again — up, down, up (bullish) or down, up, down (bearish). Both sides have now been offered at those prices, so the area is considered "balanced" and finished. The practical consequence: a gap sitting underneath a balanced range can be left permanently open, because the market has no unfinished business there. That is what makes a breakaway gap a breakaway gap.

Opening gaps: NWOG and NDOG

These are not chart patterns; they are literal holes in the price series caused by the market being closed.

  • New week opening gap (NWOG) — the space between Friday's closing price and Sunday's opening price.
  • New day opening gap (NDOG) — the space between the 5pm close and the 6pm open on the futures session.

They are given unusual weight because they are genuine voids: no trading happened between the two prices, so there is no price discovery there at all. The recommended practice is to keep the last four or five weekly gaps drawn on the chart permanently, along with each one's midpoint, and to expect price to react at them even weeks later. When several of them sit close together, price often goes range-bound between them.

The event horizon is a related idea: the midpoint between two adjacent weekly gaps. Price approaching that halfway line is expected to be pulled toward the nearer gap rather than escaping.

How to draw them

Use a low timeframe such as the 5-minute chart, not the daily — on a daily chart the gap is hidden inside a single candle. Mark the two prices, then add a 50% line between them. On futures charts, use the regular-trading-hours view rather than the electronic one, or the gap will not be visible at all.

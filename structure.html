Market structure

Structure is the part most traders think they already know — higher highs, lower lows — and it is where most of the damage gets done. The version used here is stricter, ranked into three degrees, and explicitly subordinate to narrative.

What counts as a swing point

Three candles. That is the whole definition, and keeping it mechanical prevents a lot of wishful chart-reading.

  • Swing high — a candle whose high is higher than the candle immediately to its left and the candle immediately to its right.
  • Swing low — a candle whose low is lower than the candles on either side.

The colours of the three candles do not matter. Only the shape does. Note that this means a swing point is never confirmed until the candle after it has closed — you cannot mark one in real time on the current bar.

The three degrees

Once you have swing points, they get promoted by the same rule applied recursively. This is what turns a messy chart into a hierarchy.

DegreeDefinitionRead as
Short-term (STH / STL) A basic three-candle swing point Noise, unless it is being used for entry timing
Intermediate-term (ITH / ITL) A short-term low with higher short-term lows on both sides (and the mirror for highs) The level that matters. Breaking one is a real structural event
Long-term (LTH / LTL) An intermediate-term low with higher intermediate-term lows on both sides The frame for the whole move. Usually visible on the daily chart

Displacement

Displacement is the quality control on every structural event. It means a move that is fast, large relative to recent candles, and closes decisively beyond a level — leaving a fair value gap behind it.

The purpose of the concept is to separate two things that look similar on a chart: price drifting through a level, and price being driven through it. Only the second is treated as evidence that a large participant is involved.

Displacement presentDisplacement absent
Large-bodied candles, small wicks Small candles, long wicks, indecision
A clear close beyond the level A wick through the level, then a close back inside
A fair value gap is created No gap — everything is overlapping
Read as: the move is real, take the signal Read as: the level held. This is a reversal clue, not a failure

Lack of displacement is also a signal

This is the part people miss. If you are bullish and price dips below a short-term low but does it limply — no big candles, no close below, price immediately back inside the range — that failure to displace is itself an entry cue. Somebody absorbed the sell orders. The mirror applies when bearish: a high gets tagged, nothing happens, price falls back. That is a short signal, not a warning.

Market structure shift vs break of structure

The two terms are used loosely everywhere, including by people who should know better. The distinction that is actually useful:

Break of structure (BOS) — price continues the existing pattern: a new high in an uptrend, a new low in a downtrend. Confirmation of what is already happening.

Market structure shift (MSS) — price breaks the pattern in the opposite direction, with displacement, shortly after a liquidity raid. This is the reversal signal.

The rule that saves the most money

Structure means nothing without narrative. If you cannot say which pool of liquidity price is travelling toward and where it is in the premium/discount range, then a broken high or low tells you nothing. Reading structure in isolation is exactly how traders end up buying the top of a raid because "it broke out".

The three drives pattern

A simple exhaustion read. Price pushes toward a level in three successive attempts, each one making a slightly further extreme. The idea is that each push consumes the short-term orders above it, so by the third one there is very little fuel left.

Two useful notes. First, the third drive does not have to reach the target level — if it stalls short and reverses hard, that is a stronger signal, because it says there was nothing left to take. Second, the pattern's real function is as an early warning: if price is grinding toward a pool without the three-drive rhythm, expect a straight stop-run instead.

Either way, the trade is the same: wait for displacement away from the level, find the gap that displacement left, enter there.

The market's three phases

Underneath all of this sits an old accumulation/manipulation/distribution model:

  1. Accumulation. Quiet, range-bound price while a position is built without moving the market against itself.
  2. Manipulation. A deliberate push the wrong way — false breakout, stop run, news spike — to trap participants and collect the last fills.
  3. Distribution. The real move, ending where the position is unloaded into the traders chasing it.

There is an ordering rule attached that is worth remembering because it constrains what you should expect next: consolidation is always followed by expansion. After an expansion you get either a retracement or a reversal — but you do not get consolidation straight back to consolidation. The timed version of this cycle is on the time page.

How a directional bias is actually built

Bias is not a feeling you have before the open. It is the answer to a short, fixed list of questions, asked in order, mostly on the daily chart.

  1. What just happened? Did price take a significant pool of liquidity? Did it reach a significant gap? Was there a structural break with displacement?
  2. Where is the next draw? Are there clean highs or lows sitting exposed? Is there an unfilled gap that needs rebalancing? Name one destination.
  3. Premium or discount? Inside the current dealing range, is price expensive or cheap? Only buy in discount, only sell in premium.
  4. Does the previous day agree? Was the previous day's high or low taken? Did price fail to displace past it? Failure to displace beyond a previous day's extreme is a classic reversal frame.
  5. Is there a correlated market confirming? See SMT divergence below.

Two disciplines make the difference between a bias and a guess. Pick a direction and trade only that direction for the session — if you are bearish, only look for shorts. And accept that being bullish does not mean buying every day; it means waiting for price to reach a discount array at the right time of day, which may not happen.

SMT divergence

SMT — "smart money technique", though it is usually just called SMT divergence — is a correlation check. Take two markets that normally move together, such as the S&P and Nasdaq futures, or EUR/USD and GBP/USD. At a moment when you expect a turn, compare them:

  • One makes a higher high, the other fails to — bearish. The one that could not follow is showing weakness, and the one that did is showing a false break.
  • One makes a lower low, the other holds — bullish, same logic inverted.
  • For inversely correlated pairs (a dollar index against an index future), you compare in the opposite sense.
Use it as a confirmation, never as a trigger

Divergences appear all over the chart and most mean nothing. The correct use is narrow: you have already decided a level matters, price arrives there, and then you check whether the correlated market failed to follow. That failure is the green light. Scanning for divergences and trading them on sight is a reliable way to lose money.

Order flow as a live check

Once you are in a position, there is a simple ongoing test of whether the premise still holds:

  • Bullish: down-close candles should act as support. Every time price dips into one and turns higher, the read is intact.
  • Bearish: up-close candles should act as resistance. If price closes decisively above one, the read is in trouble.

This is the same logic as an order block, applied continuously rather than at one level. It also gives you a clean, non-emotional exit rule: when the candles that are supposed to hold stop holding, the idea is finished.

Top-down analysis

The routine that stitches the timeframes together. Three roles, and each one does exactly one job:

RoleDay trader's chartJob
Higher timeframeDaily / 4-hourDirection and the destination level. Nothing else.
Intermediate1-hour / 15-minuteFind the specific array to trade and manage the position.
Lower timeframe5-minute / 1-minuteTiming the entry and spotting the first sign it is over.

One practical stopping rule for drilling down: work 5-minute, then 4, 3, 2, 1 — and the moment you find a usable fair value gap, stop. Do not keep descending. If there is no gap by the 1-minute chart, there is no trade.

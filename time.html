Time

A price level with no time attached is close to useless, and a time window with no level at it is just a clock. The claim underpinning this whole section is that the same level behaves completely differently at 3am and at 3pm, because different participants are awake.

All times are New York time

Every window below is quoted in New York local time, which is the reference clock the entire methodology uses. If your platform runs on exchange time, broker time, or UTC, convert once and write the result down — a one-hour error here silently invalidates everything on this page, and the daylight-saving changeovers in the US and Europe do not happen on the same dates.

The five killzones

A "killzone" is a window when a session opens or two sessions overlap, and therefore when volume arrives. The framework does not treat every hour as tradeable — most of the day is explicitly written off.

Asia · 20:00–00:00

Usually a consolidation, and that is its value: it gives you a small, well-defined range whose edges become the first targets when London arrives. Draw the highest high and lowest low of the window and extend both lines forward. A narrow Asian range — on the S&P futures, roughly 10 to 15 points — is read as a coiled spring and a good sign for a trending day. A wide one (over about 15–20 points) is a reason to skip London entirely and wait for New York.

London open · 02:00–05:00

The highest-energy window of the day and, statistically, the most likely place for the day's high or low to be set. The expected sequence on a bullish day: London raids the Asian high first (the false start), then reverses down through the Asian low into a discount level, and that low becomes the low of the day. On a bearish day, the mirror. The London range — 02:00 to just before the New York open — should be measured and kept, because the next day's retracement often lands inside it.

New York open · 07:00–10:00

The easiest session to read, because London has already done the hard part: the day's high or low is usually already in place, so New York is either a continuation of London or a clean reversal. It is a reversal specifically when London ran into an untouched higher-timeframe level and crossed into New York sitting there. Otherwise, expect continuation, entered on a pullback into a gap or block on the 5- or 15-minute chart.

Key moments inside the window: 08:30 when scheduled US data is released and the news embargo lifts; 09:30 when the equity market opens and volatility spikes. If big news lands at 08:30, do not also expect a fake-out at 09:30 — the manipulation already happened. If news is scheduled for 10:00, expect the fake-out to come just after it rather than at 09:30, and stay out of the first ten minutes either way.

London close · 10:00–12:00

London traders square up and New York goes to lunch, which produces profit-taking and often a retracement off the day's extreme. On a day that has already run a long way — more than about five times its recent average daily range — expect roughly a fifth of the day's range to be given back in this window. It is also where a day's direction can genuinely flip, so a position held through it deserves attention.

Central bank dealers range · 14:00–20:00

Late afternoon into the Asian handover. Its use is as a measuring stick rather than a trading window — see below.

The daily rhythm

Stitching the sessions together produces a repeating shape for the day. It will not be exact, but it gives you an expectation to check against:

  1. 20:00–00:00 Asia consolidates. The range forms.
  2. 00:00–02:00 Price drifts off the midnight opening price — the first manipulation.
  3. 02:00–05:00 London expands. The high or low of the day is set.
  4. 05:00–08:00 Back to consolidation. A dead zone.
  5. 08:00–08:30 A retracement, setting up the morning.
  6. 08:30–11:00 The main New York move: continuation or reversal.
  7. 11:00–13:30 Lunch. Consolidation, or a reversal of the morning.
  8. 13:30–16:00 The afternoon session, sometimes repeating the morning's move in miniature. Acceleration into the close around 15:40–15:50 as market-on-close orders land.

Hours of operation

A short list of intraday ranges worth having drawn, because each one becomes the liquidity target for a later window:

RangeWindowWhy it matters

Lunch range12:00–13:30Usually consolidation. Its edges get raided later.
PM session range13:30–16:00The previous day's PM high and low are prime targets the next morning.
London range02:00–05:00Raided by New York if the morning does not reach the PM range.

The routine that uses them: in the first thirty minutes after the opening bell, ask where price sits relative to yesterday's PM range and this morning's London range. Whichever is closer is the liquidity in play. Then wait for displacement between 10:00 and 11:00.

Power of three

The accumulation / manipulation / distribution cycle, applied to a single candle at any timeframe. Every candle has an open, and price spends the period first drifting one way, then going the other way for real.

Applied to the day

Mark the midnight opening price and leave it on the chart. If your bias is bullish, you want the manipulation to happen below it, typically between 08:30 and 09:45, and you want price to reclaim and hold above it before you commit. Bearish is the mirror. For the afternoon, the 13:30 opening price is used the same way.

Applied to the week

Mark Sunday's opening price and extend it to Friday. On a bullish week, expect the low to form between Monday and Wednesday — most often Tuesday or Wednesday — below that opening price, and expect price to expand away afterwards. Once it has clearly expanded away past Wednesday, it usually does not come back. If it does come back through, expect consolidation or a reversal for the rest of the week.

One practical note that comes up repeatedly: Monday's extremes are treated as untrustworthy. The preference is to let Monday build its range, then look for the real weekly high or low on Tuesday or Wednesday at a key level.

Central bank dealers range

A measuring tool built from the 14:00–20:00 window. The method:

  1. On the 1-hour chart, mark the high and low of 14:00–20:00. Use candle bodies, and check the wicks separately.
  2. Mark the midpoint.
  3. Project the range's height above and below it as a series of equal steps — one deviation, two deviations, and so on.
  4. On a sell day, the day's high tends to form around one deviation above the range, and the day's low around two deviations below it. On a buy day, the mirror.

Conditions that make it work better: the range itself under about 20 points on the S&P futures, an Asian range of 10–15 points, and mid-week rather than Monday. High-impact news pushes price to larger deviations, and a move out to a fourth deviation is usually associated with either big news or a full New York reversal profile.

What this really is

Strip the language away and it is a volatility-normalised projection: measure a quiet overnight range, assume the day expands to a multiple of it, use those multiples as targets. That is a legitimate and testable idea. It also means the specific point values quoted here are instrument-specific and era-specific — they were calibrated on index futures at a particular level of volatility, and blindly reusing them on another market or in a different regime will not work.

Day of the week and time of day

  • The weekly high or low most often forms Tuesday or Wednesday, frequently before the New York open.
  • The best trending days tend to be Monday through Wednesday; that is when the framework prefers to take positions.
  • For a sell day, the high of the day usually lands in the 02:00–05:00 window and the low around 10:00–11:00. Buy days are the mirror. That pairing is also an exit rule: if you sold from London, plan to be finished around 10–11am.
  • The 05:00 opening price is worth marking. If London gave no false start, the fake-out often appears here instead.
  • 07:20 is when index futures activity picks up ahead of the cash open, and is a reasonable point to start hunting New York setups.

Days to stay out

The framework is unusually explicit that some days are not worth trading, which is a useful corrective to the assumption that every session owes you a setup.

DayWhy
FOMC decision daysTwo-stage event at 14:00 — the first move after the release is routinely a fake, and the press conference frequently reverses it. Conditions are unlike any other day.
Thursday and Friday of payrolls weekAimless chop while the market waits, then a violent release. Low-quality conditions in both directions.
The day after a very large expansionThe move already happened. Expect consolidation or an aimless sweep of both sides.
Holiday-thinned sessions, late DecemberLiquidity leaves; levels behave unreliably.
After the weekly objective is metIf the level you named as the week's draw has been hit, the incentive is gone.

The tell that you are in a low-quality environment: price ranges back and forth with no clean follow-through, and levels that normally produce reactions are traded straight through. That is what "high-resistance liquidity conditions" means in practice — precision degrades, and the correct response is to stand aside rather than to trade smaller.

Seasonality

A rough calendar map, used as background context rather than as a signal:

  • December–January — consolidation; holidays drain liquidity.
  • February–April — the market comes out of its range; generally the cleanest trending conditions of the year.
  • May–August — profit-taking, then a summer lull. August is the quietest.
  • September–November — activity returns and the cycle restarts.

Treat these as tendencies with wide error bars. Their only real use is as a tiebreaker when your analysis is already pointing one way.

Quarterly shifts and IPDA data ranges

IPDA stands for interbank price delivery algorithm — the framework's name for whatever mechanism is assumed to be delivering price. Its practical output is a look-back exercise:

  1. On the daily chart, counting weekdays only, mark the highest high and lowest low of the last 20, 40 and 60 trading days.
  2. Inside each look-back window, note the untouched highs and lows, gaps, voids and blocks.
  3. Take the 60-day high and low, cut that range in half, and mark the premium and discount arrays on each side.
  4. Then look forward the same distances. The claim is that the algorithm will reach for something within the next 20, 40 or 60 trading days from the most recent structural shift.

Wrapped around this is the "quarterly shift" idea: every three to four months, the market changes character — a trend pauses into consolidation, or a range breaks. The purpose is not prediction of direction but calibration of when to expect something significant, and how far back your reference levels should reach.

Read this critically

The IPDA framing asserts a specific mechanism — a single algorithm delivering price to predetermined levels — that has no public evidence behind it. You do not have to accept the mechanism to use the exercise: "mark the extremes of the last 20, 40 and 60 sessions and know where the untouched levels are" is sensible housekeeping regardless of what you believe is moving price. Keep the housekeeping; hold the story loosely.
